Rockstar used to make games that dared you to be offended. Now? Rockstar posts a Halloween promo about a driverless car, and plenty of fans reply by asking for GTA news instead.

So what happened to the studio that once turned its own scandal into a statue?

Let's go back. Grand Theft Auto III gave us Donald Love, a real estate tycoon that critics and fans read as a Trump parody, with a cannibalism habit. Vice City had about three times as much talk radio as GTA III, built on exaggerated callers and the style of host Lazlow Jones. San Andreas had eleven radio stations and more than three times as many in-game commercials as GTA III, and Dan Houser said the target was the broader weirdness of American consumerism and action movies.

Then GTA IV. A lawyer named Tom Goldberg, written as a jab at Jack Thompson, the real lawyer who spent years going after video games. Rockstar took the guy attacking them and put him in the game. That is not a studio asking for permission. And when the Hot Coffee scandal dragged politicians into it, Rockstar's reply in GTA IV was a Statue of Liberty parody holding a coffee cup. Subtle, it was not.

GTA V gave us Jock Cranley, a washed-up action star running for governor, a Schwarzenegger parody. And LifeInvader, which, yes, is Facebook with the serial numbers filed off.

Notice the pattern. Nobody was safe, including Rockstar itself. The old Rockstar picked a target, aimed, and fired. It had a personality, and the personality had teeth.

Now, the modern Rockstar. In 2018, Dan Houser said he and the senior writing team had worked 100-hour weeks. He later clarified it was a four-person team, for three weeks, not years. Fine. But that was the first time many fans heard how the legend was built.

And then came October 2025.

More than thirty Rockstar employees were dismissed, according to reporting. Rockstar, importantly, never confirmed that number. It said it acted against, quote, "a small number of individuals" for sharing confidential information in a public forum. It said, quote, "This was in no way related to people's right to join a union or engage in union activities."

The union, the IWGB, says that forum was a private Discord, and calls the firings, quote, "plain and simple union busting."

Here's what we actually know. The union filed legal claims for victimisation, collective dismissal and blacklisting. In January, a tribunal refused the workers interim relief, saying it could not conclude it was likely that union membership was the principal reason. That was not a final ruling. In June, the tribunal refused to throw out the blacklisting claims. And this week, in Glasgow, the closing arguments wrapped up. Rockstar's lawyer said the workers were "caught red-handed." The claimants' lawyers said management acted as union membership approached ten percent. Those are claims. Neither side has been proven right yet, and the judge has said she cannot give a timeframe for a judgment.

So no, we are not going to pretend we know the motive. A judge will decide that. But look at the timing. Rockstar says it dismissed people over what was shared in a Discord, while the biggest game of its life was in the pipeline, and now a union vote is running while the game is out. That is not exactly the vibe of a studio that doesn't take itself seriously.

And the voice has changed too. Bloomberg reported that in 2020 Rockstar quietly shelved a planned GTA Online mode, Cops 'n' Crooks, over how it would look during the debate about policing. That's reported by Bloomberg, from unnamed sources. Companies are allowed to be careful. But the old Rockstar built its name by not being careful.

Which brings us to GTA VI. It came out on the nineteenth, and the early reviews are glowing. But reviews are not the question. The question is whether it's funny. Rockstar North's co-studio head Rob Nelson said they have to be careful that what they satirize does not feel dated, and that very specific political issues or memes will date quickly. A Rockstar senior vice president of narrative, Rupert Humphries, said they avoid simply mirroring reality, because you could just go outside and live in the real world. Smart? Sure. Safe? Also yes.

Reportedly, the new approach avoids specific real people and movements. That's a choice. It might be a good one. But satire that never names anybody is just a vibe.

So did Rockstar change? Fans say the humor got safer. And the workers say the company got harder on the people who build the games, while Rockstar says it only acted on confidential leaks. Maybe the game is brilliant. Maybe it's still got bite. I can't tell you that.

What I can tell you is that this studio built its legend by throwing punches. So tell me in the comments. Did Rockstar change for the worse, or are we just older?
